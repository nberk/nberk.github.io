---
title: "The Next Frontier in Legal AI: Getting the Facts"
date: 2026-10-19
tags: ["AI", "law", "legaltech"]
---

The legal industry is paying close attention to how quickly AI is getting better at completing legal work. Benchmarks track complex situations in which the models are given documents and facts and asked to produce legal work product.

The industry is paying far less attention to an equally important step: getting the facts in the first place.

Firms often spend as much effort gathering information as analyzing it. Whether you’re preparing a will, drafting a brief, or negotiating a contract, you need information from outside sources.

As AI gets better at producing legal work, gathering the information it needs becomes increasingly important. If AI can draft a brief in minutes, but obtaining the underlying records takes three weeks, the records are now the bottleneck.

Firms that have robust, efficient processes for collecting information and getting it into a form AI can use will benefit from improvements in the underlying models much more than firms that have not invested in streamlining information intake.

It helps to divide the information into three categories, roughly from easiest to most difficult to automate: information in systems firms can already access, existing records held by others, and information that hasn’t been documented and must be obtained through conversations with people.

Technology is progressing on all three fronts, but each presents different challenges. And the last goes to the heart of the profession: when will clients want a human involved, and what are they really paying their lawyer for?

## Giving AI access to information is getting easier

The good news is AI tools are already making it much easier for AI to access information that is either public or stored in tools firms use.

This has mostly taken the form of “connectors” and “plugins” that integrate with the other tools lawyers use. Anthropic recently added plugins and integrations with tools like Westlaw, CoCounsel, and Harvey. OpenAI’s “Astra for Law” launched 26 partner-built plugins, including iManage, Clio, and Thomson Reuters.

Increasingly, lawyers will no longer need to copy and paste information from these tools. AI will get the information automatically.

Firms should monitor which integrations are available; these connectors are the simplest way to give AI access to information. This is the lowest-hanging fruit.

## Obtaining information from others will get easier

Now consider information held by others. Sometimes that’s an individual - a witness with photographs of an accident, or a former employee with relevant documents. Other times it’s an institution, like a hospital, bank, or government agency.

Obtaining this information can be a mess, particularly when dealing with institutions. Collecting the necessary records can involve a maze of authorizations, calls, faxes, and proprietary portals, often paired with weeks of follow-ups.

I expect progress here, but it will likely be uneven.

In the short term, firms can use AI to help automate the record requesting process. That can be as simple as asking AI to help research how to request information from a particular provider, and to fill out whatever forms are necessary. AI voice agents are improving rapidly, and soon it will be easy to ask your AI agent to call a provider and request a record (that’s already possible today, but it requires stitching technology together - it will soon be available out of the box).

Companies are popping up to productize the most time-consuming record retrieval tasks. Companies like [Codes Health](https://www.getcodeshealth.com/) and [Tavrn](https://www.tavrn.ai/) are automating medical record retrieval. [Andco](https://www.useandco.com/)’s agents can also retrieve police reports.

For now, these tools often work by mimicking humans: logging into portals, sending faxes, placing calls.

But, in the long term, as AI speeds up everything else, institutions will face more pressure to provide information to agents directly. There are issues to work out, particularly how to electronically verify requests. But there will be progress over time.

## Getting undocumented information will be trickier

A lot of information only exists in someone’s head.

Some of it is factual: a business process that isn’t written down, a recollection of what happened. Some of it is softer: a client’s goals, their risk tolerance, what they’d settle for.

So how will AI get information that hasn’t been documented?

The answer depends partly on what AI can handle, and that’s improving quickly.

AI can already draft and send emails and questionnaires requesting information. Attorneys should review these requests before they go out, but over time, attorneys will review less and less. And many firms are already using AI for intake.

I’m not going to comment on the current quality of any particular technology. But it is safe to say that AI is, or soon will be, capable of basic fact gathering (via a chatbot or voice agent). You’ll be able to specify the information you want the agent to collect, and it can handle the back and forth.

And before long, AI agents will be capable of even more nuanced, open-ended conversations, working out on the fly what other facts may be relevant to the matter. It may be as simple as asking an agent to “gather all facts relevant to claims the client may have.” Given that they can do the research in parallel, they may be even better at identifying potentially relevant facts! We’re already seeing this type of benefit in depositions: AI tools suggest follow-up questions to attorneys in real time, flagging inconsistencies and gaps in testimony.

Privilege is a real obstacle. There are unresolved questions about how attorney-client privilege applies when a lawyer uses an AI agent to interview a client. The answer may depend on how the tool is deployed, who can access the conversation, etc. Firms will need to monitor and think carefully about these issues. But I expect the answers will become clearer in the next few years.

## When do people want to interact with a human?

Even if AI becomes capable of having sophisticated interviews, will people actually want to talk to it?

This is particularly important when it comes to clients. When will clients be comfortable discussing their legal problems with AI, even knowing a lawyer will review everything?

That question goes to the heart of what lawyers do. Are clients paying lawyers just to produce and review legal work, or are they paying for something more?

Client contact isn’t just an exchange of information. It’s about building a relationship. Often what a client needs most is someone to listen.

It’s easy to say that “clients will always prefer to talk with a human” - but I’m not so sure. Competition is going to push firms to be more efficient, and AI agents may actually provide a better experience in some cases.

Advancements in technology may offer firms the ability to experiment with how they collect information. For example, firms may have AI handle the more basic “fact gathering” and allow the lawyer to have the more nuanced conversations.

Right now, my sense is the technology is probably reliable enough to handle basic fact collection and not much more. But soon, cultural expectations will be the bigger limiting factor.

## My ultimate advice: be intentional about how you collect and store information

The lesson for firms is to think systematically about how information enters the practice, where it’s stored, and how easily AI can use it.

This is particularly important for information gathered through conversations. At the very least, firms should develop systems to capture what they learn on client calls and put it somewhere AI can access it - even if that’s just a document in the client files.

You or your client may not be comfortable recording calls and having AI extract the relevant information. But tools that process recordings locally and delete the underlying audio may make the practice more accepted over time.

People’s preferences and expectations regarding AI are evolving quickly. The firms that succeed will be those that are closely in tune with both what the technology can do, and what their clients want - and can build systems that adapt as both change.
